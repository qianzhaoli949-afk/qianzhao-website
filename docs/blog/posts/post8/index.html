What Can a Sample Tell Us About a $50 Average?

A simulation of bootstrap intervals and hypothesis tests

simulation
statistics
data visualization
Use simulated samples to see how bootstrap confidence intervals and hypothesis tests work.
Published

October 28, 2026

Suppose a student wants to estimate average weekly spending on groceries. They ask 50 people and find that the sample average is close to $50. How much should they trust that number? A different group of 50 people would probably give a different average. A confidence interval and a hypothesis test give us two ways to describe that uncertainty.

I use a simulated example so we can see the truth that real survey data usually hide. I generate individual spending from a normal distribution with a mean of $50 and a standard deviation of $10. These values are assumptions for a teaching example, not actual grocery data. The charts start with 50 people; use the menu to compare samples of 20 and 100. Each choice shows a separately generated sample and a fresh set of simulations saved with the post.

1. Build an interval from one sample

First, I draw one sample from the population. To bootstrap it, I draw the same number of observations from that sample with replacement. Some people can appear more than once in a resample, while others do not appear at all. I calculate the mean each time and repeat this process 500 times. The middle 95% of those bootstrap means, between the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles, gives a percentile bootstrap interval.

For the sample of 50, the mean is $50.32, and the interval runs from $47.64 to $52.99. The histogram shows how the mean changes when we reuse the observed sample. The interval is not a range containing 95% of individual grocery bills; it describes uncertainty about the population mean.

2. Repeat the whole exercise

One interval cannot tell us whether the procedure works in the long run. Here I draw 1,000 independent samples from the population and construct a new bootstrap interval for each one. The chart displays the first 100 intervals. A teal line contains the true mean of $50; an orange line misses it. The count below the chart uses all 1,000 intervals, including those not shown.

With 50 people, 945 of the 1,000 intervals contain $50: 94.5%. That is close to 95%, although it need not equal exactly 95% in a finite simulation. With 20 people, the same run covers $50 in 92.7% of intervals. Simulation noise and the percentile method’s finite-sample accuracy can both contribute to the difference. A 95% confidence level describes the performance of the procedure across repeated samples. Once we compute one interval, it either contains the fixed population mean or it does not; it is not correct to assign that particular fixed interval a 95% probability of containing the fixed mean.

3. Test a $50 mean

Now consider a different question: would the observed sample mean be unusual if the population mean really were $50? My null hypothesis is that the mean is $50. For this teaching example, I also assume the population distribution is normal with a known $10 standard deviation. I simulate 5,000 new sample means under those assumptions. The orange tails mark the most extreme 5% of the simulated absolute deviations from $50, split between the two sides.

The solid line shows the observed sample mean. I calculate a two-sided p-value as the fraction of null simulations at least as far from $50 as that mean. For 50 people, it is about 0.820, so this example does not reject the null at the 5% level. A p-value is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true. It measures how unusual the sample result would be under the stated null model. Both the simulated cutoff and p-value have some Monte Carlo variation; a different random seed can change them slightly.

Takeaway

The bootstrap uses one sample to estimate uncertainty, while repeated sampling lets us check how often the interval procedure covers a known truth. A hypothesis test asks a related but different question: how unusual is the observed result under a particular null model? Try changing the sample size and compare the widths and p-values. The sample changes too, so the p-value need not move in one direction. The near-95% coverage here depends on this simulation setup and does not guarantee the same coverage for every population or bootstrap method.
